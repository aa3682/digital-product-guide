# Business Setup

This area covers what the law treats you as once you sell, the name you sell under, what an entity does and does not do, the permits a city or county may require, and the rules your day job may impose on outside work. None of it blocks a first sale; these are the rules that start applying the day money arrives.

## Key ideas

### You are already a business

Selling for money makes you a [sole proprietor](/glossary#sole-proprietor): one person running a business, with no legal line between the person and the business. Nothing is filed to become one; it is the default until you form something else. Income is reportable from the first sale, whether or not anything was registered. [Taxes and Records](/domains/taxes-and-records) covers what that reporting looks like.

Priya's checklist sells its first copy on a Tuesday evening. On Wednesday she is a sole proprietor with one sale to record. Nothing else changed.

### The name you sell under

Selling under your own name needs nothing. Selling under any other name, such as a shop name or a name for the product line, usually needs an assumed-name filing, also called a fictitious name or doing-business-as registration. It is filed with the state or the county, and the office and the fee vary by state; see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#business-setup) for where to look.

An assumed-name filing tells the public who stands behind the name. It does not give you the name. A federal trademark is a separate, optional step through the federal trademark office, with its own application and cost. The two answer different questions: one says who you are, the other says who may use the mark.

### What an entity does and does not do

An [entity](/glossary#entity) is a legal form the state recognizes as separate from its owner. For a one-person business, the common one is a limited liability company. Forming one costs a state filing fee, and many states also require an annual report or a yearly fee to keep it in good standing; both vary by state, and [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#business-setup) points to the source.

What the entity does is separate the business's obligations from the owner's personal assets. If a refund dispute becomes a claim, or a rights holder says the product used their material, the claim is against the business rather than the person's savings. That separation is what an entity is for. Insurance is another route to similar protection, and a seller can hold either, both, or neither.

What a one-owner entity does not do by default is change federal income tax. The owner still reports the business on their personal return, on the same schedule a sole proprietor uses. Corporations and tax elections change that picture, and they are beyond a first product.

### Permits and licenses

A city or county may require a general business license from anyone doing business there, including from a kitchen table. Some localities have separate rules for home-based businesses, which rarely touch a digital product. A sales tax permit is a different document: it registers you with the state to collect sales tax, and where you must collect, the permit comes before the first collection. All three vary by state and locality; [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#business-setup) names where to check.

### Keep the money apart

A bank account used only for the business, open by the first sale, is what makes every later record possible. The [storefront](/glossary#storefront) and [payment processor](/glossary#payment-processor) accounts go in the name of whatever the business is, your own name for a sole proprietor or the entity's name once one exists, and payouts land in the business account.

For an entity, this is more than tidiness. The liability separation depends on the business being run as something separate. Personal bills paid from the business account, or business costs paid from a personal card with no record, are what a court looks at when deciding whether the separation was real. Mixing the money undermines the protection the entity exists to provide.

### Your job's rules

An employment agreement often covers outside work. Common clauses restrict competing with the employer, restrict use of the employer's time, equipment, or accounts, and claim ownership of what an employee makes in certain circumstances. Some employers require disclosure or approval of any outside business, and some regulated professions require it regardless of employer. Your contract and your field decide which apply. The ownership question also runs through [Copyright and Licensing](/domains/copyright-and-licensing), because a product built from employer material has a rights problem before it has a business one.

## Common misconceptions

- That an entity is required before selling. Selling makes you a sole proprietor by default; an entity is a choice, not a prerequisite.
- That an entity lowers income tax by itself. A one-owner entity is taxed like a sole proprietor unless a separate election is made.
- That registering a business name reserves it. An assumed-name filing identifies you to the public; only a trademark gives a right to exclude others from a mark.
- That a side project is a hobby rather than a business. Selling for money is a business activity from the first sale.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
The obligations this area leaves running are the annual report or fee that keeps an entity in good standing, the renewal on any local license, and keeping the name on every account matched to whatever the business is. If you have been selling without attending to them, check first whether last year's report or renewal was filed; a lapsed entity can lose the separation it was formed for without anyone telling you. Then reread any employer disclosure against what you sell today, since a disclosure that described a checklist does not cover a membership. Last, confirm the legal name and payout account on the storefront and processor match the entity, not the person who set them up.
</Callout>
