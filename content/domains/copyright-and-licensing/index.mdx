# Copyright and Licensing

This area covers who owns what you make, what a buyer actually receives when they pay, how material that is not yours can and cannot go into your product, and what happens when someone copies you.

## Key ideas

### Copyright arrives with the work

[Copyright](/glossary#copyright) is the set of exclusive rights the law gives the author of an original work: to copy, distribute, adapt, display, and perform it. It exists the moment original expression is fixed in a form that can be read back, such as a saved file, with no notice and no registration. Priya owns the copyright in her checklist the evening she saves it.

Registration with the Copyright Office is a separate, optional step with a fee. It is required before a court case over a U.S. work, and a registration made within the window the statute sets makes statutory damages and attorney fees available. The fee and the window are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#copyright-and-licensing). A copyright notice is also optional, and still useful: it removes a copier's claim not to have known.

### What copyright does not cover

Copyright protects expression, not the thing expressed. Ideas, methods, systems, facts, titles, short phrases, and blank forms are not protected. A workbook's method can be used by anyone; the words and arrangement that teach it are yours. A template protects its text and layout, not the concept.

This cuts both ways: it limits what you own and what you can stop. Someone who teaches the same method in their own words has not copied you; someone who reproduces your pages has.

### Work other people did for you

A hired writer, designer, editor, or narrator owns the copyright in what they contribute unless one of two things happens. The first is that the contribution is a [work made for hire](/glossary#work-made-for-hire), whose copyright belongs to the person who commissioned it from the start. For a contractor that is a narrow category: the work must be one of the types the statute lists and be covered by a written agreement, signed by both sides, that calls it a work made for hire. The second is a written assignment transferring the copyright to you.

Stock images, fonts, music, and templates come under their own licenses. The [license](/glossary#license), not the purchase, decides whether commercial use, redistribution, and inclusion in a product for sale are allowed. The rights list you keep in [Make the Product](/process/make-the-product) is the record of all of this.

### What the buyer gets

A sale transfers a license, not the copyright: the buyer receives permission to do certain things with the work, and you keep the work. The usual shape for a downloadable product is use by the buyer, no redistribution, and no resale. A course or membership sells access for a period, not ownership of the material inside. A template can be licensed for the end product the buyer makes with it while resale of the template stays reserved. A separate commercial tier can allow more.

The license is written into the terms of sale, where the buyer agrees to it. What those terms must contain belongs to [Terms, Refunds, and Privacy](/domains/terms-refunds-and-privacy).

### Using material that is not yours

Someone else's material can appear in your product in four ways. [Public domain](/glossary#public-domain) material, where copyright has expired or never applied, can be used by anyone. Material under an open license can be used on the conditions the license attaches: credit, no commercial use, or the same license on anything built from it. [Fair use](/glossary#fair-use) allows some quotation and reuse without permission, but it is a defense decided case by case on four statutory factors, and no percentage or word count makes a use fair; the Copyright Office's [Fair Use Index](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/) collects the court decisions. Another company's brand name can be mentioned to describe compatibility or a comparison, but not in a way that suggests the brand made or endorses your product.

### Material made with generative tools

The Copyright Office's stated position is that output a generative tool produces without sufficient human authorship is not protected by copyright, and that a registration application must disclose such material. Your own selection, arrangement, and editing of that output can be protected; the raw output is not.

### When someone copies you

Most hosts and marketplaces run a takedown process built on the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA). A notice goes to the host's designated agent, listed in the Copyright Office's directory, and must contain the elements the statute lists: identification of the work and of the copy, contact details, statements of good faith and accuracy, and a signature. The host removes the material and tells the person who posted it, who can answer with a counter-notice; the material then goes back up unless a court case follows, and that case requires registration.

## Common misconceptions

- That registration or a notice is required for protection. Copyright exists when the work is fixed.
- That changing enough of someone else's work makes it yours. An adaptation needs the owner's permission, however much was changed.
- That educational use is automatically fair use. Purpose is one of four factors, and none decides alone.
- That buying a template or stock file transfers its copyright. It transfers a license, which sets the limits.
- That free to download means free to sell. A free file still carries its owner's terms, which often forbid resale.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
This area leaves three things running. The rights list is kept current as the product is updated, since each new image, quote, or contributor adds a line. Third-party licenses are kept within scope as sales grow, since some cap the end products or sales they cover. And registration is done before a dispute rather than after, since the remedies that make a case worth bringing depend on when it was filed. If you have been selling without these, check first whether every contributor's assignment or work-for-hire agreement is in writing, then whether every stock license permits sale inside a product.
</Callout>
