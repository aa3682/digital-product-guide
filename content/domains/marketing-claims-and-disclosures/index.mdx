# Marketing Claims and Disclosures

This area covers what you can say about your product and its results, what testimonials and reviews require, when a relationship with someone promoting the product has to be disclosed, and how a disclosure has to look before it counts. The rules apply wherever a product is described, not only on the sales page.

## Key ideas

### The baseline

The FTC Act bars unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce, and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) enforces it. A claim is deceptive when it is likely to mislead a reasonable buyer about something that matters to their decision. The claim can be express, in the words on the page, or implied, in what words and images together suggest. Every claim needs [substantiation](/glossary#substantiation): evidence the seller holds before the claim is made, adequate to what the claim says. State consumer-protection laws run alongside and are enforced by state attorneys general.

### Results and income claims

A statement about what a buyer will get is read as a claim about what the typical buyer gets, unless the ad clearly and conspicuously says otherwise. Income, time saved, grades, weight, a job: each is a results claim. A line saying "results not typical" does not cure it. The seller needs evidence of what the typical buyer actually gets. Claims about making money draw the most enforcement, and the FTC has put sellers on notice about them, with the penalty it can seek per knowing violation after such a notice listed on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#marketing-claims-and-disclosures).

Take Priya's onboarding checklist. She can say what it contains, that it covers the paperwork for a first hire, and that it is for new supervisors, because each is true and she can show it. She cannot say that it will get a buyer promoted, because she holds no evidence about what happens to buyers. The first describes the product; the second promises a result.

### Testimonials and reviews

A testimonial is a statement by a buyer or user about their experience. It must reflect the honest experience of a real user, and any result it describes is a claim the seller must be able to back. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials makes specific practices eligible for [civil penalties](/tools/this-years-figures#marketing-claims-and-disclosures): fake or generated reviews, paying for reviews conditioned on their sentiment, undisclosed reviews by the seller's own people or relatives, suppressing negative reviews by threat, and faking social-media indicators of influence. An incentive for a review is allowed only when it is not tied to the review's sentiment and is disclosed.

The Consumer Review Fairness Act bars form terms that stop buyers from leaving an honest review or penalize them for it. How that limits the terms of sale is in [Terms, Refunds, and Privacy](/domains/terms-refunds-and-privacy).

### Material connections

An [endorsement](/glossary#endorsement) is any message a buyer would take as the opinion of someone other than the seller. When the endorser has a [material connection](/glossary#material-connection) to the seller, a relationship the audience would not expect that could affect how they weigh the endorsement, it must be disclosed. An affiliate commission is one. So is a free copy, a payment, a job, or a family or close personal tie. The seller is responsible for what its affiliates and endorsers say and for whether they disclose; a seller's own affiliate program therefore carries an obligation to police both.

### How a disclosure has to look

A disclosure counts only when it is [clear and conspicuous](/glossary#clear-and-conspicuous): hard to miss and easy to understand for an ordinary member of the audience. That means it appears in the same medium as the claim, close to it rather than at the bottom of the page, without a click or a scroll to find it, and in plain words rather than a hashtag buried in a list. A disclosure can qualify a claim by saying what it does and does not cover. It cannot fix a claim that is false.

### Free, guarantees, prices, and scarcity

Four words carry rules of their own. "Free" means free: any condition on the offer is disclosed up front, next to the word. A money-back guarantee must be honored as stated, with its conditions disclosed before the sale. A crossed-out former price is a claim that the product actually sold at that price. A deadline or a limited quantity must be true, and a deadline that quietly extends was not. The [launch discount](/glossary#launch-discount) in [Launch](/process/launch) is this rule in practice.

### Where claims live

Advertising is wherever the product is described to a buyer: the sales page, every email, every social post, and the listing on a marketplace. A marketplace's listing rules add to the law and can be stricter. Email carries its own requirements, covered in [Email and Audience Rules](/domains/email-and-audience-rules).

## Where it shows up in the process

- [Choose the Product](/process/choose-the-product): The one-sentence promise chosen there is the first claim made about the product, and the product has to deliver it.
- [Make the Product](/process/make-the-product): The description written when the product is finished becomes the sales page, so it must match what the buyer receives.
- [Set the Price](/process/set-the-price): A former price shown beside the current one, and any guarantee attached to it, are claims.
- [Launch](/process/launch): The launch message is the first sustained set of claims about the product, and its discount and deadline are claims too.
- [Keep Selling](/process/keep-selling): Testimonials, reviews, and affiliates arrive here, and each brings a disclosure obligation with it.

## Common misconceptions

- That a disclaimer fixes a misleading claim. A disclosure can qualify a true claim; it cannot make a false one true.
- That "results not typical" makes an income claim safe. The seller still needs evidence of what typical buyers get.
- That a friend's honest praise needs no disclosure. Honesty is one requirement; the connection is another, and it must be disclosed.
- That reviews on a seller's own site can be curated to the positive ones. Hiding negative reviews because they are negative misrepresents what buyers said.
- That "free with purchase" is free. An offer that requires a purchase has a condition, and the condition must be disclosed with the word.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
This area leaves three things running. Substantiation is kept on file for every claim in current use. Affiliate agreements require disclosure and bar claims the seller cannot back, and affiliates' posts are checked against them. And review practices are kept matched to the rule, including how incentives are offered and what happens to negative reviews. If you have been selling without these, check first whether any live sales page states a result you cannot back with evidence about typical buyers, and whether your affiliates' posts carry a disclosure.
</Callout>
