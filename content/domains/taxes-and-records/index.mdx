# Taxes and Records

This area covers the federal taxes a sale creates, how the income reaches your return, the forms a payment processor may send, sales tax on digital products, and the records that make all of it possible. The obligations here run on dates, whether or not anything sold that month.

## Key ideas

### Income tax on what you keep

The business's net profit goes on Schedule C, filed with your personal return. Net profit is receipts minus the ordinary and necessary expenses of running the business: the storefront's cut, software used to make and deliver the product, and similar costs. Refunds reduce receipts. Sales tax collected from a buyer is never income; it passed through you on its way to the state. What is left joins your other income on the return. State income tax applies too, under each state's own rules.

Take Priya's checklist with round numbers. A year of sales brings in two thousand dollars. The storefront kept two hundred, she refunded one hundred, and the tool she built the file in cost one hundred. Her Schedule C shows sixteen hundred of profit.

### Self-employment tax

Net earnings from self-employment above a small yearly threshold also owe self-employment tax, which funds Social Security and Medicare the way payroll withholding does for an employee. It is figured on Schedule SE and paid with the same return, on top of income tax. Part of it is deductible when figuring income tax. The threshold and the rate are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#taxes-and-records).

### Paying as you go

No one withholds tax from a sale. Federal tax is due through the year, and once the tax you will owe for the year passes a threshold, quarterly [estimated tax](/glossary#estimated-tax) payments on Form 1040-ES are expected on set dates. Missing them can add a penalty even when the return is paid in full. Both the threshold and the dates are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#taxes-and-records).

A reader with a job has a second route. Raising withholding at work on Form W-4 sends more of each paycheck where the estimated payments would go, and many people with side income find that simpler.

### The forms a processor sends

A [payment processor](/glossary#payment-processor) or [marketplace](/glossary#marketplace) reports your gross payments to the IRS and to you on Form 1099-K once those payments pass a reporting threshold. The threshold has changed in recent years; [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#taxes-and-records) carries the current one. The form shows gross: what buyers paid, before fees and refunds. Schedule C is where those come out, which is why the two numbers should differ and why records showing the difference matter. The income is reportable whether or not a form arrives.

### Sales tax on digital products

Sales tax is a state and local tax on the buyer, collected by the seller. Whether a state taxes digital goods at all varies by state; some tax a downloaded file as they tax a printed book, some do not, and some treat courses and memberships differently from files. Whether you owe a state depends on the buyer's location and on [nexus](/glossary#nexus), the connection between a seller and a state that lets the state require collection. A seller with no physical presence in a state can still have economic nexus once sales into that state pass the state's threshold, and each state sets its own; see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#taxes-and-records).

Most states have [marketplace facilitator](/glossary#marketplace-facilitator) laws, under which a marketplace collects and remits sales tax on behalf of the sellers using it. On your own [storefront](/glossary#storefront), the tool may calculate and collect the tax at checkout, but registration and filing remain yours.

### What to keep and for how long

Keep the sales and [payout](/glossary#payout) statements from the storefront or processor, every refund record, each expense receipt, the rights list from [Make the Product](/process/make-the-product), and a copy of every return filed. The IRS describes how long to keep records, and the period depends on what the record supports; see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#taxes-and-records).

A separate bank account and a simple ledger do most of the work. The ledger is a list of each sale, refund, and expense with a date and an amount, updated on a [cadence](/glossary#cadence) rather than reconstructed before filing. At year end it should reconcile to the processor's statements and to the form the processor sent.

### Hobby or business

The IRS distinguishes an activity carried on to make a profit from a hobby, and the distinction decides whether expenses are deductible. A business deducts its ordinary and necessary expenses; a hobby reports income without them. The IRS weighs how the activity is run rather than applying a single test, as its page on [the difference between a hobby and a business](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/know-the-difference-between-a-hobby-and-a-business) describes.

## Where it shows up in the process

- [Set the Price](/process/set-the-price): The net from a sale, after the storefront's cut and sales tax, is what you plan around and what you report.
- [Set Up the Shop](/process/set-up-the-shop): The payout account and the sales tax setting on the storefront are where the records begin.

## Common misconceptions

- That no form means no tax. Income is reportable from the first sale, with or without a form.
- That small side income is exempt. A small amount may fall below a reporting threshold and is still income on the return.
- That the platform handles all of your taxes. A marketplace may collect sales tax; income tax and self-employment tax are always yours, and on your own storefront so is sales tax registration.
- That sales tax collected is yours until you file. It belongs to the state from the moment it is collected.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
The obligations this area leaves running are the estimated payments on their dates, a yearly reconciliation of payouts to the income reported, and a watch on nexus as sales spread across states, since a state whose threshold you crossed sends no notice. If you have been selling without doing these, check first whether the income on last year's return matches the processor's form, and if the numbers differ, whether fees and refunds explain the gap. Then check whether estimated payments were made, and if not, whether withholding at a job covered them. Bring the ledger current first; every other check depends on it.
</Callout>
