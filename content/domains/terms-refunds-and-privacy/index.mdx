# Terms, Refunds, and Privacy

This area covers the three documents a buyer can read before paying: the terms of sale, the refund policy, and the privacy notice. It explains what each must say and which federal and state rules decide that.

## Key ideas

### The terms are the contract

The [terms of sale](/glossary#terms-of-sale) are the agreement between you and the buyer. Terms shown before payment and accepted by an affirmative click form a contract, and the E-SIGN Act means a contract or signature cannot be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic. Terms shown only after checkout, or only in a footer, are weaker: the buyer can argue they never agreed.

The terms say what is sold; the license the buyer receives, which [Copyright and Licensing](/domains/copyright-and-licensing) explains; how delivery and access work; the refund policy; a plain statement that the product is educational and results are not guaranteed; and which state's law governs. State law limits how one-sided terms can be; an unfair clause can be set aside while the rest stands.

### Refunds

No federal rule creates a refund right for a digital product. The [refund policy](/glossary#refund-policy) you post, state consumer-protection law, and the rules of the [storefront](/glossary#storefront) or [marketplace](/glossary#marketplace) decide together what a buyer can get back. Whatever it is, it must be shown before purchase and honored as written. A no-refund policy is generally allowed for a digital good when it is disclosed conspicuously before the sale; some states set posting requirements for such a policy, found at the state's consumer protection office.

A refund policy binds you and the buyer, not the buyer's card issuer, so a buyer can still dispute the charge; what that costs is in [Payments and Chargebacks](/domains/payments-and-chargebacks). Buyers outside the United States may have withdrawal rights under their own country's law. Priya's policy is a full refund on request within two weeks, in the same words on the page and in the terms.

### Subscriptions and memberships

A [negative option](/glossary#negative-option) is an offer where silence counts as yes: the charges continue until the buyer acts to stop them. A [membership](/glossary#membership) billed each period is one; so is a free trial that converts to paid unless cancelled. The Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act sets three conditions for any such offer made online: all material terms disclosed clearly before billing information is collected, the buyer's express informed consent before any charge, and a simple way to stop the charges. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has published an enforcement policy statement on how it reads them.

Many states add automatic-renewal laws with their own rules on notice at signup, reminders before renewal, and cancellation; those vary by state. A membership run on a course platform still answers to them, whatever the tool's checkout does.

### The privacy notice

The [privacy notice](/glossary#privacy-notice) tells buyers what personal information the shop collects and what happens to it. It says what is collected: name, email address, purchase history, and whatever the analytics record. Why. Which services receive it: the [payment processor](/glossary#payment-processor), the storefront, the email service, and analytics. How long it is kept, and how a buyer can ask about it. Card numbers are held by the payment processor, never by you, and the notice can say so.

California's online privacy statute requires any commercial site collecting personal information from the state's residents to post a privacy policy, which in practice means every shop. The comprehensive state privacy laws, which add duties beyond a posted notice, apply only above thresholds of revenue or number of consumers; those vary by state and are on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#terms-refunds-and-privacy). A first product usually sits below them. The posted notice is required either way.

### Children

The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) applies when a product is directed at children under the age it sets, or when the seller knows it is collecting personal information from them; the age is on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#terms-refunds-and-privacy). Once it applies, a separate and demanding set of rules follows, starting with verifiable parental consent before any collection. What triggers it is the subject matter, the language and images, and who the product is marketed to.

### Security and breaches

Collecting only what the shop needs, and deleting what it no longer needs, limits what can leak. Every state has a breach-notification law: if buyer data you hold is exposed, notice to the affected people is required on the state's terms and timeline, which vary by state.

### Where the documents live and whose rules govern

The terms and the privacy notice are linked from checkout, where the buyer accepts them, and from the footer. On a marketplace, the marketplace's own terms govern the buyer relationship and yours supplement them. On your own storefront, the tool may supply default terms and privacy notice that bind you until changed. Email consent is its own area, [Email and Audience Rules](/domains/email-and-audience-rules).

## Common misconceptions

- That a copied template covers any shop. It describes another product's license, refunds, and services.
- That "all sales final" prevents chargebacks. A refund policy binds the buyer, not the card issuer.
- That a small shop needs no privacy notice. It is required from the first sale that collects a name or email address.
- That the storefront's or marketplace's privacy policy covers you. It covers what that company collects, not what you do.
- That a free trial is not a subscription. A trial that converts to paid unless cancelled is a negative option.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
This area leaves three things running. The terms and privacy notice are kept matched to what is sold and collected. Renewal notices go out on whatever schedule applies where you sell. And all three documents are re-read whenever a membership tier or a new tool is added, since each changes what is promised or who receives data. If you have been selling without these, check first whether the posted refund policy matches the dispute settings at the processor, then whether the privacy notice names every service receiving buyer data.
</Callout>
