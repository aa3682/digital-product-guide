# Choose the Product

This step is a decision, not a build. You pick one problem you already solve for other people, choose a format that fits it and your hours, check that the result will be yours to sell, and write the promise in one sentence. A [digital product](/glossary#digital-product) is a file a buyer downloads or an online space they pay to enter.

## Why it matters

Every later step takes this decision as given. The price depends on what the product fixes and for whom. The shop depends on the format, because a file and a members-only space are delivered differently.

Skip it, and the cost shows up as rework. The most common failure is a product nobody asked for, discovered after weeks of evenings went into it. The next is a product that never finished, because it grew past what a person with a job could ship.

## What actually happens

### Start from a problem you already solve

Look at the questions people already bring you: the email you have answered five times, the explanation you give every new colleague, the spreadsheet a friend asked to copy. Each is proof that a problem exists and that people want it solved by you. Write them down before you write anything else.

A product built from that list has buyers before it exists. A product built from a topic you find interesting has to go and find them. Pick the problem that comes up most often and that you can describe in a sentence.

### Pick a format that fits the problem and your hours

The [format](/glossary#format) is the shape the product takes. This guide covers five: ebooks, workbooks, templates, courses, and memberships. The first three are files a buyer downloads and keeps. Courses and memberships are [access products](/glossary#access-product): the buyer gets entry to a space you keep running. A [membership](/glossary#membership) is the ongoing version, where the buyer pays on a schedule and you owe something new each period.

| Format | What the buyer gets | Build, then upkeep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ebook | A written guide to read and refer back to | Weeks of writing and editing. Upkeep: near zero. |
| Workbook | Exercises and prompts they fill in themselves | Weeks of writing and layout. Upkeep: near zero. |
| Template | A ready-made file they adapt to their own use | Days to weeks of building and testing. Upkeep: help when the file confuses someone. |
| Course | Lessons in order, with exercises | Months of recording or writing. Upkeep: hosting, updates, and student questions. |
| Membership | Ongoing access to new material, a community, or you | Weeks to open. Upkeep: new material every period, for as long as it runs. |

A problem with a fixed answer fits a template or a workbook, which are close to finished when they ship. A problem that takes practice fits a course. A membership fits a problem that keeps changing, and it turns you into a publisher on a schedule. For a first product alongside a job, favor the formats that are done when they ship.

### Check that you can own it

You can only sell what is yours to sell. Work you made yourself, on your own time and outside what your job pays you for, is usually yours. Anything from someone else needs a right you can point to before it goes in: text you didn't write, images you found, code you copied, a template a colleague built, course material you made for an employer. Employer work catches professionals most often, because it feels like theirs and often isn't. List each borrowed piece as you plan, and read [Copyright and Licensing](/domains/copyright-and-licensing) before you build around any of it.

### Size it to finish

The first product is the one you can finish and ship in weeks alongside a job, not the flagship. If the plan needs a year of evenings, it is a second or third product. Cut it until a version you would be willing to sell fits in a handful of weeks.

A small product that ships teaches you who buys, what they ask, and what to make next. A large one that stalls teaches nothing. Take Priya, a payroll manager whom every new supervisor asks about onboarding paperwork. Her first product is a ten-page checklist and one form template, not the full course she has in mind. It ships in three weeks and tells her which part of the course people would pay for.

### Write the promise in one sentence

Write one sentence with three parts: who it is for, what it fixes, and what they get. For Priya: "For new supervisors onboarding their first hire, a checklist and form set that gets the paperwork right the first time." If you can't fill all three parts, the product isn't chosen yet.

Then test the sentence on three to five people who match the "who". Read it to them, and ask whether it describes a problem they have. You are asking, not selling. No payment is collected at this step and no delivery is promised. What you want is the wording they use; it feeds the next step.

## Questions to ask yourself

- What have you explained more than twice this year, and to whom?
- Can you say who this is for in a way that excludes most people?
- Which parts of the planned product came from somewhere other than you?
- Did anyone who matches the reader tell you the sentence describes their problem?

## Common mistakes

- Starting from a topic instead of a problem; interest on your side is not demand on theirs.
- Choosing a membership first because it sounds like recurring income; it is recurring work before it is recurring income.
- Reusing work from your job without checking who owns it; the problem surfaces after launch.
- Skipping the test conversations; the wording buyers use is launch copy you don't have yet.

## Which business areas apply

- [Business Setup](/domains/business-setup): The format sets how much of an ongoing operation you are starting, which shapes how you set up.
- [Copyright and Licensing](/domains/copyright-and-licensing): Anything you didn't make needs a right you can point to, and that check happens here, before it is built in.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Your second product starts from records, not guesses. Pull the questions buyers sent, the refund reasons, and the requests that came in more than once; that list is the demand check, already done. Choose the problem that shows up most, and write the promise in the buyers' own words. Check the format against the obligations you already carry: if you run a membership, a downloadable product asks less of you than a second membership would. Write down what the first product taught you about who bought, so the next choice is documented rather than remembered.
</Callout>
