# Launch

This step is the announcement. You pick a day, tell the people who already know you about the product in plain words, and spend the first week watching what happens when strangers try to buy it.

## Why it matters

A first launch is a test with money in it. The promise test asked whether people had the problem; the launch asks whether they will pay to fix it. Everything after this step, from the next version to the next product, is built on what the first week shows.

Skip the discipline and the launch either never happens, because the product is always one fix away from ready, or it happens as a stream of posts nobody asked for. The quieter failure is a launch that says more than the product can back up. A claim made on launch day is one you answer for later.

## What actually happens

### Pick a day and one message

A launch is a day and a message. Pick a day when you can watch the shop and answer email, not one spent traveling or in meetings. Then write one message. Its material is the wording people used at the promise test: how they described the problem and what they wanted from the fix. Priya's message opens with the sentence two supervisors used, "I never know which forms have to be done before day one," and says the checklist answers it.

### Tell people in order

Tell the promise-test and test-read people first, by name, one at a time; they know the product and some of them shaped it. Next, contacts who match the reader. Then wherever you already talk to people, a newsletter, a professional group, a place you post. No paid advertising for a first product; you do not yet know which words sell it, and paying to broadcast the wrong ones teaches nothing.

An [audience](/glossary#audience) is the group of people who agreed to hear from you. That agreement is the line. Write to people who asked to be written to, give each message a way to stop, and leave everyone else alone. What counts as agreement, and what a message must carry, is set out in [Email and Audience Rules](/domains/email-and-audience-rules).

### Say what is true and nothing more

The message has five parts: the promise, what is inside, the price, the refund promise, and the link. That is the whole of it. Leave out results you cannot back up, such as what buyers will earn or save. Leave out urgency that is not real; a deadline that quietly moves is one people remember. Leave out anyone else's name or words without their consent, test readers included. Where the line falls is in [Marketing Claims and Disclosures](/domains/marketing-claims-and-disclosures). Priya's message names the ten-page checklist, the one form, forty dollars, the two-week refund, and the link.

### A launch discount, if any

A [launch discount](/glossary#launch-discount) is a lower price for a stated period around the launch. It needs two true things: a reason you can say in a sentence, and an end date. It ends when you said it would, at full price, with no extension. If you cannot name the reason, do not discount; the price was chosen for a reason in Set the Price and does not need help on day one. Priya offers no discount, because she has no reason for one, and her readers already told her the price reads as small.

### The first week

Watch two things: support and the buying flow. Fix stalls the day they appear. Refund fast and without argument; a refund you argue over becomes a chargeback, and the argument costs more than the sale. Write down every question anyone asks, in their words. That list is the raw material for the next step.

A first launch is for proof, not income: proof that the promise sells to people who are not your friends. In round hypothetical numbers, Priya writes to forty people over the week. Nine buy, one asks for a refund, and three ask the same question about a state form. Nine sales are not a business. They are proof that supervisors she has never met will pay for the checklist, and a note on what the next version needs.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Who told you they had this problem, and have you told each of them first?
- Does every sentence in the message describe something a buyer will actually receive?
- If there is a discount, can you say its reason and its end date in one sentence?
- What did the first week's questions and refunds tell you?

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for one more fix; the product was finished when you would have taken money for it.
- Writing to people who never agreed to hear from you; it costs trust before it costs anything else.
- Promising results; a buyer who did not get them has a claim, and you have no answer.
- Extending the discount; every later deadline is read as negotiable.
- Reading nine sales as failure; the first launch measures whether the promise sells, not how much.

## Which business areas apply

- [Terms, Refunds, and Privacy](/domains/terms-refunds-and-privacy): The refund promise in the message is the one on the terms page, and the two must match.
- [Payments and Chargebacks](/domains/payments-and-chargebacks): The first refunds happen this week, and a fast one is the cheapest outcome.
- [Email and Audience Rules](/domains/email-and-audience-rules): Who you may write to, and what each message must carry, is decided before the first one goes out.
- [Marketing Claims and Disclosures](/domains/marketing-claims-and-disclosures): Every sentence in the launch message is a claim you answer for.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
Launching to an existing audience means writing two messages, not one: past buyers already trust you and need to know what is different, while prospects need the promise. Before you announce, honor what the last product's price included; if it promised updates, buyers of it are owed those before they are asked to pay again. Reread every claim still standing on the old sales page, because the new launch draws attention to it. Decide how a launch discount sits with what existing buyers paid, and say so to them first.
</Callout>
