# Set the Price

This step puts a number on the product. You decide what the product is worth to the buyer, learn what part of the price reaches you, choose one round number, and write down what it includes.

## Why it matters

The shop is built around the price and what it includes. A single file at one price is a different setup from a membership billed each month. The launch announces it, and the refund promise is written next to it.

Skip it and one of two things happens. The price is set to cover what the product cost to make, which for a digital product is the wrong question. Or it is set low because low feels safe, and the first sales show what the storefront and the state keep.

## What actually happens

### Price is a claim about value, not a cost recovery

A physical product has to recover what each copy costs to make. A digital product costs nearly nothing to deliver one more of. The hours you put in are spent whether it sells ten copies or a thousand. What sets the price is what the problem is worth to the buyer: the time it saves, the mistake it prevents, the thing it lets them do.

Priya's checklist took three weeks of evenings, and that number tells her nothing about the price. What a supervisor's first onboarding mistake costs, in a missed deadline and a payroll correction, does.

### Anchor to the alternative

Ask what the buyer would do without the product. Spend an afternoon working it out, hire someone, buy a larger course, or do it wrong and fix it later. The price sits well below that alternative and clearly above free. That is the [anchor](/glossary#anchor): the comparison the buyer makes when they see the number.

For Priya's buyer the alternative is a morning of searching and a call to payroll, or a mistake found at the first pay run. Against that, forty dollars reads as small. The anchor comes from the promise test, where people told you what the problem costs them.

### Know what you keep

The [list price](/glossary#list-price) is the number on the sales page. It is not what reaches you. The storefront or payment processor keeps a cut of each sale, and any sales tax collected from the buyer was never yours; it passes through you to the state. What is left after both is your [net](/glossary#net), the figure to plan around.

Walk Priya's product through it with round hypothetical numbers. A buyer pays forty dollars. The storefront keeps a few dollars as its fee. Where the buyer's state taxes digital products, tax is added on top and passed to the state, either by you or by the storefront on your behalf. What is left, a little under the forty, is what pays for Priya's time. For what a storefront or payment processor keeps, see [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#payments-and-chargebacks), and for sales tax on digital products, see [the taxes section](/tools/this-years-figures#taxes-and-records).

### Pick one price and write down the reason

A first product gets one price. Make it a round number you can say without a pause. Write the reason under it in a sentence: what the alternative costs and why this sits where it does.

No ladder of tiers unless the product splits into two things a buyer would see as different, such as the checklist alone and with the form. No reflexive discount. A launch discount is a decision for the [Launch](/process/launch) step, made for a reason and a set time; it is not part of the price. Priya's note reads: "Forty dollars. Below a morning of a supervisor's time, above what a free search would make anyone trust."

### Decide what the price includes

The price buys a defined thing. Decide whether it includes updates when the file changes, whether the buyer can ask you questions and for how long, and, for a membership, how long a period runs, what a buyer pays yearly against monthly, and what canceling means. Write each answer down; it becomes the terms.

The refund promise is set alongside the price. A price you are confident in is easier to refund, because you expect to do it rarely. Decide the window and conditions here; the policy itself belongs to [Terms, Refunds, and Privacy](/domains/terms-refunds-and-privacy). Priya's price includes the current version and any update in the next year, and a refund within two weeks, no reason needed.

## Questions to ask yourself

- What would the buyer do without this, and what does that cost them?
- After the storefront's cut and sales tax, what reaches you from one sale?
- Does the product genuinely split into two things, or is the second tier padding?
- What does the price include, and for how long?

## Common mistakes

- Pricing from your hours; the buyer never sees them.
- Planning around the list price; the storefront's cut and sales tax come out before anything reaches you.
- Launching with three tiers; a first product rarely splits, and the tiers split the buyer's attention.
- Discounting at the start out of nerves; the price then has to be raised later, in public.

## Which business areas apply

- [Taxes and Records](/domains/taxes-and-records): Sales tax collected from a buyer is not yours, and the net from each sale is what you record and report.
- [Terms, Refunds, and Privacy](/domains/terms-refunds-and-privacy): What the price includes and the refund promise are decided here and written into the terms.
- [Payments and Chargebacks](/domains/payments-and-chargebacks): The cut a storefront or payment processor keeps decides what the price leaves you.
- [Marketing Claims and Disclosures](/domains/marketing-claims-and-disclosures): Showing a former price next to the new one, or attaching a guarantee to it, turns the price into a claim you must be able to back.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
A price change starts with today's cuts, not the ones from when the price was set. Recompute the net at today's storefront fee and tax treatment before you choose the new number; the gap between list and net drifts. Write down the reason for the change in one sentence, so you can explain it to a buyer who asks. Decide before you announce what happens to existing members and past buyers: whether they keep the old price, for how long, and whether a past buyer gets the next version. A price announced before those answers exist gets set by the first complaint.
</Callout>
