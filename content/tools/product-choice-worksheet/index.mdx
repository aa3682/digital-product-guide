# Product Choice Worksheet

This worksheet belongs to [Choose the Product](/process/choose-the-product), the first step of The Launch Path. It walks through the same decisions that step describes, in order, so that by the last question you have a one-sentence promise ready to test on real people. Nothing here gets built; it is a decision made on paper.

## How to use it

Print it or copy the questions into your own notes and answer them in writing, because an answer you can read back is one you can test. Every number you write is your own: hours, people asked, weeks to finish. Any number set by law, an agency, or a platform lives on [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures), and this page links to it where one comes up.

## Audience

A [digital product](/glossary#digital-product) sells to a person you can describe. Start with who already asks you for help.

1. Who asks you for help, and what do they have in common: a role, a stage, a situation?
2. Can you describe that person in a way that excludes most people you know?
3. How do you reach them today: by email, at work, in a group you belong to?
4. Name three to five people who fit the description and would take a call from you.

Example: Priya's audience is new supervisors handling their first hire, a group she meets every month through her payroll work.

## Problem

1. What have you explained more than twice this year, and to whom?
2. Which of those explanations comes up most often?
3. What does it cost the person when the problem goes unsolved: time, money, a mistake found later?
4. Write the problem in one sentence, in the words the last person used when they asked.

Example: Priya's problem is a supervisor who does not know which forms must be done before a new hire's first day.

## Format

The [format](/glossary#format) is the shape the product takes, and it sets how much upkeep you take on. A file a buyer downloads is done when it ships. An [access product](/glossary#access-product), a course or a [membership](/glossary#membership), keeps you working after the sale.

1. Does the problem have a fixed answer, need practice, or keep changing?
2. Which format fits that: a template or workbook, a course, or a membership?
3. How many hours a week can you give this, and for how many weeks?
4. What is the smallest version you would be willing to sell?
5. Which pieces of the planned product came from somewhere other than you, and what right do you hold to each?

Example: Priya picks a checklist and one form template, sized to ship in three weeks of evenings, and leaves the full course for later.

## Evidence of demand

Interest on your side is not demand on theirs. Demand is a person with the problem saying so.

1. How many times has someone brought you this problem in the past year?
2. What do people do about it now, and what does that cost them?
3. Has anyone offered to pay, or asked whether a written version exists?
4. Which three to five people from your audience list will you read the promise to?
5. What did each of them say, in their own words?

Example: Priya counts eleven supervisors who asked about onboarding paperwork last year, and two of them asked whether she had a written version.

## The one-sentence promise

A promise has three parts: who it is for, what it fixes, and what they get. If any part is blank, the product is not chosen yet.

1. Who is it for?
2. What does it fix?
3. What do they get?
4. Write the three parts as one sentence.
5. Read it to the people on your list. Did they say it describes a problem they have?
6. Which words did they use that you did not? Keep them; they become the sales page.

Example: "For new supervisors onboarding their first hire, a checklist and form set that gets the paperwork right the first time."
